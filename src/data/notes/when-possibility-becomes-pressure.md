Every week there is something new in AI.

A new model comes out. A coding agent gets better. Someone finds a new way to build with it. Then I open social media and people already have demos, tutorials, opinions, products, and screenshots of their profit or MRR—all from the thing I have barely read about.

I am interested in all of it. That is part of the problem.

Possibility has always been exciting to me. When I encounter a new idea or capability, my mind immediately starts asking what it could become, what it could connect to, and what I might be able to make with it.

That instinct is a big part of why I build. But lately I have been noticing the other side of it. When new possibilities arrive faster than I can explore them, they stop feeling entirely exciting. They start competing with one another.

A product can feel worth pursuing in the morning, then somehow feel outdated after twenty minutes of scrolling. I start wondering whether I should change the model, learn a new framework, rebuild the workflow, or work on a completely different idea that now seems more possible than it did yesterday.

Nothing about my original work changed during those twenty minutes. What changed was the number of other futures I could suddenly imagine.

I do not consider myself successful yet. I am still learning how to build well, especially with agents. I am learning how to turn an experiment into a product. I am also learning distribution: how to explain what I made, put it where people can find it, and give it a real chance to become useful.

So I am not writing this from the position of someone who has figured out how to focus while everyone else gets distracted. I am writing from inside the distraction.

When people talk about keeping up with AI, it can sound like one learning problem. For me, it is at least three problems happening at once. I am learning the technology. I am learning how to make a product with it. I am learning how to distribute that product.

Each one can take all the time I give it.

I looked through the official release logs because I wanted to know whether the pace only felt ridiculous from inside social media. It does not. Across the AI platforms and coding tools that a builder might use together, there are multiple potentially relevant updates in an ordinary week.

But the logs also made something else clear: “new” is a very broad category. A release can make a previously impossible product possible. It can improve quality, lower cost, or force a migration. It can also be a minor interface change, an early preview, or a feature that is genuinely impressive but unrelated to the problem I am working on.

The feed presents all of them as new. Social media presents many of them as urgent. Neither one knows what I am trying to do.

My goals have to make the final distinction.

I think possibility without direction eventually becomes pressure. Every new development offers another path, but I cannot walk all of them at the same time. If I have no clear reason for choosing one, every unchosen path can start to look like a mistake.

Social media makes that feeling sharper because I am not only seeing the possibility. I am seeing another person apparently realize it. They have already built the demo, launched the product, found an audience, and posted the MRR. I am still deciding what to learn next.

I know those posts are selected parts of someone else's work. I do not see the abandoned versions, the experience they had before starting, the help they received, or the rest of their circumstances. Knowing that helps, but it does not automatically stop me from comparing.

I still have to notice when I have stopped learning from another person and started using their progress to judge my own direction.

This is where I think some security in yourself matters. Not the security of feeling ahead. Not the confidence of believing your current idea will definitely work. I mean having somewhere stable enough to return to before allowing new information to change the plan.

What am I trying to build? Who is it for? What am I trying to learn through it? What is actually preventing me from moving it forward?

Sometimes a release will change those answers. That is one reason to keep paying attention. But I do not want every release to ask the questions again from the beginning.

I also do not want “stay grounded” to become an excuse to stop exploring. Curiosity is not the enemy here. I still want to learn new techniques, test better tools, and let the field change what I think is possible.

The challenge is learning how to stay open without becoming directionless.

I am starting to think the work itself can help. A real product gives my curiosity a place to land. If I am struggling with unreliable outputs, I have a reason to learn evaluation. If cost is becoming a problem, I can investigate models, caching, or routing. If people do not understand why the product is useful, another week of technical research may not be the learning I need. I may need to learn distribution instead.

This does not mean building automatically turns into learning. I can ask an agent to generate a lot of code without understanding why it works or where it will fail. The feature moves forward, but I may not become more capable.

Learning still asks me to pause, make a prediction, inspect the failure, and explain what changed. But I also cannot wait until I understand everything before shipping. There will always be another model, framework, paper, or workflow that makes waiting feel responsible.

Distribution interrupts that loop. It places the product in front of people who are not inside my head. Their reaction can tell me what I need to improve, what I need to explain, and whether the possibility I chose matters outside my own imagination.

I do not know the perfect balance between learning, building, and distributing. I doubt they need equal time. What seems important is that they continue to inform one another instead of becoming separate places where I hide from the next uncomfortable step.

Maybe what I need is not less curiosity. Maybe I need enough commitment to let one possibility become concrete before I replace it with another.

“Let’s see what this could become” is how I naturally approach ideas. I usually think of it as an invitation to begin: follow the curiosity, open the question, try the thing.

I am realizing that it also asks me to stay.

I cannot see what something could become if every new release persuades me to abandon it before it has time to become anything. At some point, possibility needs attention, effort, and the courage to let other people encounter it.

I am still learning how to do that. I still get pulled into release notes. I still compare myself with people who seem further ahead. I still confuse preparation with progress sometimes.

Being grounded may not mean knowing which future will work. Maybe it means choosing one possibility and staying with it long enough to see what it could become.
